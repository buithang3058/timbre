# Justin Welsh Templates

Swipe file of Justin Welsh's post frameworks — the solopreneur playbook behind 500K+ LinkedIn followers and The Saturday Solopreneur. Best for: personal-brand posts, solopreneur/creator topics, milestone stories, and systems content.

## How to Use This Guide (Agent Instructions)

1. **Avoid First-Match Bias:** Consider all templates here plus `linkedin-frameworks.md` and `post-structures.md` before selecting. Welsh templates shine when the concept has a personal story, a number, or a system behind it.
2. **Templates are skeletons, DNA is the voice.** These templates dictate *structure* (hook placement, body shape, ending type). The loaded voice DNA (`voices/bui-thang/dna.md` + `voices/[project]/dna.md`) dictates *wording, tone, and language*. When a template's stylistic tic conflicts with a DNA anti-pattern, **DNA wins** — keep the skeleton, drop the tic. See "Voice DNA Conflicts" at the bottom.
3. **One post = one idea.** Welsh's cardinal rule. If a concept needs two ideas to land, split it into two posts.
4. Examples below are in English (source material). Render final posts in the project's language per DNA.

## The 4A Content Framework

Welsh classifies every post into one of four styles. Use this to vary a content calendar and to reframe one concept four ways.

| Style | What it does | Typical templates |
|---|---|---|
| **Actionable** | Teaches a system the reader can use today | Steal My System, The Framework, The Tech Stack |
| **Analytical** | Breaks down numbers, trends, receipts | Simple Math, The Breakdown, The Receipts |
| **Aspirational** | Shows what's possible via personal story | Then vs. Now, The Origin Story, X Lessons from Y |
| **Anthropological** | Names a human pattern readers recognize in themselves | The Observation, The Hard Truth, The Permission Slip |

**Reframing move:** Take one concept and draft it once per A. A concept that is weak as Actionable is often strong as Anthropological. This pairs directly with Phase 2 volume generation.

## Post Anatomy (applies to every template)

```
[HOOK — 1-2 lines. Must work standalone above the "see more" fold]

[RE-HOOK — 1 line that raises the stakes or adds tension]

[BODY — skimmable: short lines, white space, one idea per line,
lists or short paragraphs. 5th-grade reading level.]

[END — exactly ONE of: a takeaway line, a question, or a CTA. Never stack them.]
```

**Formatting rules:**
- First 2 lines decide everything. Write 5+ hook options, pick the best.
- No warm-up, no "I want to share something today."
- Break any paragraph longer than 3 lines.
- One CTA maximum. A question OR a plug, not both.

Fill-in-the-blank openers. Pair any hook with any body template.

1. "In [year], I [low point]. Today, I [high point]. Here's what changed:"

3. "I've [done X activity] for [Y years]. Here are the [N] lessons that actually mattered:"
4. "Most people [common approach]. The top 1% [opposite approach]."
5. "Unpopular opinion: [contrarian take in one sentence]."
6. "Steal my [system/template/checklist] for [outcome]:"
7. "I wasted [time/money] on [mistake]. Save yourself:"
8. "You don't need [thing everyone chases]. You need [simpler thing]."

13. "How to [desirable outcome] (without [common pain]):"
14. "[Impressive person/group] all share one habit:"
15. "I studied [N] [examples]. The pattern was obvious:"

## 1. The "Then vs. Now" Post

### Description

Welsh's signature transformation format. Contrasts a specific dated low point with the present, then credits the change to a small set of learnable decisions. Aspirational.

### Example

```
In 2018: burned out medical sales executive, panic attacks, golden handcuffs.

In 2024: $8M+ earned as a one-person business, working 6 hours a day.

What changed wasn't luck. It was 3 decisions:

1. I built an audience before I needed it.
2. I productized one skill instead of selling my hours.
3. I published every single day, even when nobody read it.

The gap between those two lives was smaller than I thought.
It was just uncomfortable to cross.
```

### Template

```
In [year]: [specific low point — role, feeling, numbers].

In [current year]: [specific high point — numbers, freedom].

What changed was [N] decisions:

1. [Decision + why it mattered]
2. [Decision + why it mattered]
3. [Decision + why it mattered]

[One grounded closing observation — not a slogan]
```

### How It Works

- Dated contrast creates instant narrative tension in 2 lines
- Specificity (numbers, job titles) makes it credible, not humble-braggy
- Attributing change to decisions (not luck/talent) makes it actionable for the reader
- Works for any before/after: career, product, channel growth, health

## 2. The "X Lessons from Y" Milestone Post

### Description

Anchors a list of lessons to a concrete milestone (revenue mark, anniversary, follower count, post count). The milestone is the proof; the lessons are the value.

### Example

```
I've published 1,400 consecutive days on LinkedIn.

7 lessons from never missing a day:

1. Consistency beats intelligence. My smartest posts rarely won.
2. Nobody remembers your bad posts. Post anyway.
3. Formats are learnable. Voice takes years. Start now.
4. Engagement in the first hour is mostly your relationships, not your writing.
5. One clear idea outperforms three good ones in the same post.
6. Your best content is buried in what you already know and find boring.
7. The algorithm changes. Psychology doesn't. Write for humans.

Most people quit at day 30. That's the moat.
```

### Template

```
I [hit specific milestone with number].

[N] lessons from [the journey]:

1. [Lesson — punchy claim + one line of context]
2. [Lesson]
3. [Lesson]
...

[Closing: one observation about why most people never get here]
```

### How It Works

- The milestone number in the hook does the authority-building
- Each lesson must stand alone — readers screenshot individual lines
- Odd numbers (5, 7, 9) feel curated, not padded
- Reusable at every milestone: same skeleton, new number

## 3. The "Steal My System" Post

### Description

Pure Actionable. Gives away a complete, numbered, immediately usable process. Generosity is the strategy — the post is a free sample of your expertise.

### Example

```
Steal my 4-step system for writing a week of content in 60 minutes:

Step 1: Pick ONE pillar topic (10 min)
Choose from your 3-5 core themes. Don't browse for inspiration.

Step 2: Brain-dump 10 one-line ideas (15 min)
No editing. Bad ideas welcome. Volume first.

Step 3: Match each idea to a proven format (15 min)
Story, list, contrarian take, observation. Don't invent structures.

Step 4: Draft the 5 best, schedule, walk away (20 min)
Done beats perfect. You'll edit tomorrow with fresh eyes.

I've used this exact system for 5 years.
It's boring. Boring scales.
```

### Template

```
Steal my [N]-step system for [specific outcome in specific timeframe]:

Step 1: [Action] ([time/effort])
[One line of how or why]

Step 2: [Action]
[One line]

Step 3: [Action]
[One line]

[Proof line: how long you've used it / what it produced]
```

### How It Works

- "Steal" removes friction — explicit permission to copy
- Time-boxing each step makes it feel executable, not theoretical
- Sub-lines under each step separate this from a shallow listicle
- The proof line at the end converts a tip into a tested system

## 4. The "Simple Math" Post

### Description

Analytical. Reduces a business model or life decision to arithmetic anyone can verify. The math IS the hook — numbers stop the scroll.

### Example

```
Solopreneur math:

$150 digital product
× 2 sales per day
= $109,500 per year

No employees.
No meetings.
No investors.

You don't need a unicorn.
You need a calculator and one useful skill.
```

### Template

```
[Topic] math:

[Price/unit]
× [volume assumption]
= [annual result]

No [expected requirement].
No [expected requirement].
No [expected requirement].

[Closing: reframe what's actually needed]
```

### How It Works

- Numbers and the × / = layout create a visual pattern interrupt
- Deliberately simple math invites "but what about..." comments — engagement fuel
- The "No X, No Y, No Z" stack contrasts the small requirement with big assumptions
- Works for pricing, savings, time budgets, content output, compounding

## 5. The "Contrarian Take" Post

### Description

Challenges advice the audience hears constantly, then backs the challenge with experience or evidence. Welsh's rule: be contrarian about the *method*, not the *goal*.

### Example

```
"Follow your passion" is terrible business advice.

I didn't start a one-person business because I was passionate about it.

I started because I wanted control of my time.

The passion showed up around month 18 —
right after the results did.

Passion isn't a prerequisite.
It's a byproduct of getting good at something useful.

Start with leverage. Let passion catch up.
```

### Template

```
"[Common advice everyone repeats]" is [bad/incomplete/backwards].

[Your experience that contradicts it — 2-3 short lines]

[The mechanism: WHY the common advice fails]

[Your replacement principle in one or two lines]
```

### How It Works

- Quoting the cliché in line 1 triggers instant recognition
- Personal experience beats abstract argument — you're a witness, not a debater
- Explaining the mechanism separates a hot take from an insight
- Attack popular *methods*, never the reader's aspirations

## 6. The "Origin Story" Post

### Description

The full narrative arc: ordinary world → breaking point → risky decision → struggle → outcome → lesson. Used sparingly (monthly, not weekly) because it's your highest-emotion asset.

### Example

```
In 2019, I sat in a parking lot outside my office and couldn't make myself go in.

On paper, I had everything. VP title. Great salary. Stock options.

In reality, I was having panic attacks on Sunday nights.

So I did the "irresponsible" thing:
I quit with no plan except one skill — writing — and 90 days of savings.

Year 1: $47K. Terrifying.
Year 2: $310K. Wait, what?
Year 5: More than my old decade of salary, combined.

I'm not special. I was just done trading my health for someone else's dream.

The scariest decision I ever made is now the most boring fact about my life.
```

### Template

```
In [year], [scene of the breaking point — specific, visual].

On paper, [what looked good].
In reality, [what was actually happening].

So I [the risky decision].

[Timeline of struggle → progress, with real numbers]

[Lesson: what it cost, what it bought — grounded, not preachy]
```

### How It Works

- Opening mid-scene (parking lot, 2am, hospital) beats opening with biography
- "On paper vs. in reality" is a built-in tension device
- Real numbers during the struggle years build more trust than the success numbers
- The arc earns the lesson; the same lesson without the story is a platitude

## 7. The "Observation" Post

### Description

Anthropological. Names a pattern in human behavior the reader has seen but never articulated. The value is recognition, not instruction.

### Example

```
I've watched hundreds of talented people stay stuck.

Almost none of them lacked skill.

They lacked the stomach to be a beginner in public.

They'd rather be privately excellent than publicly average —
so they stay private, and invisible, forever.

Meanwhile, less talented people publish, iterate, and pass them.

Visibility compounds. Perfectionism doesn't.
```

### Template

```
I've [observed group/behavior over time].

[The surprising commonality — what it's NOT]

[What it actually is — the named pattern]

[2-3 lines showing the pattern playing out]

[One-line implication]
```

### How It Works

- "I've watched/studied/met hundreds..." establishes the sample size for the claim
- Ruling out the expected cause first ("not skill") creates a curiosity gap
- Naming an unnamed pattern is the most screenshot-able move in content
- Readers tag people: "this is you" — built-in distribution

## 8. The "Permission Slip" Post

### Description

Tells readers they're allowed to skip something the industry insists is mandatory. Relief is the emotion; simplification is the value.

### Example

```
You don't need a personal brand strategy.

You need:

- One topic you can talk about for years
- One platform where your buyers already are
- One format you can produce consistently
- One offer that solves one problem

That's it. That's the strategy.

Everything else is procrastination with a dashboard.
```

### Template

```
You don't need [complex thing the industry sells].

You need:

- One [simple element]
- One [simple element]
- One [simple element]
- One [simple element]

That's it.

[Closing: name what the complex thing actually is — fear, procrastination, status]
```

### How It Works

- "You don't need X" directly counters the reader's anxiety — instant relevance
- The "One..." anaphora makes simplicity visceral, not just claimed
- Diagnosing the complex alternative ("procrastination with a dashboard") lands the punch
- Especially strong for beginner-heavy audiences drowning in advice

## 9. The "Tech Stack / Toolkit" Post

### Description

Analytical + Actionable. Lists the actual tools/resources behind a concrete result. Voyeurism into a working system — people love seeing behind the curtain.

### Example

```
My $5M one-person business runs on 7 tools:

Writing: Typefully ($12/mo)
Newsletter: ConvertKit ($119/mo)
Courses: Kajabi ($199/mo)
Payments: Stripe (2.9%)
Calendar: SavvyCal ($12/mo)
Design: Figma (free plan)
Analytics: Fathom ($14/mo)

Total: ~$360/month to run the whole thing.

The tools are cheap.
The leverage is in what you ship with them.
```

### Template

```
My [result] runs on [N] tools:

[Function]: [Tool] ([price])
[Function]: [Tool] ([price])
[Function]: [Tool] ([price])
...

Total: [monthly cost].

[Closing: deflate the tools, point at the real variable]
```

### How It Works

- The result in the hook justifies reading a list of tools
- Prices make it concrete and prove the barrier to entry is low
- The Function: Tool format is scannable in 5 seconds
- Closing prevents the wrong takeaway (tools ≠ success)

## 10. The "Costly Mistake" Post

### Description

Inverse of the lessons post: leads with what went wrong and what it cost. Vulnerability plus a price tag equals credibility.

### Example

```
I wasted 14 months building a product nobody asked for.

The mistake wasn't the code. It was the order of operations:

What I did:
Build → Launch → Search for customers

What works:
Audience → Ask → Pre-sell → Build

I had it exactly backwards.

That mistake cost me ~$120K in opportunity cost.
Reading this costs you nothing.

Build for people who already told you what they want.
```

### Template

```
I wasted [time/money] on [specific mistake].

The mistake wasn't [surface explanation]. It was [real explanation]:

What I did:
[wrong sequence/approach]

What works:
[right sequence/approach]

[The cost, stated plainly]

[One-line prescription]
```

### How It Works

- Admitting cost buys the right to prescribe — teacher's scars, not preacher's rules
- The wrong-vs-right sequence contrast is the actual teachable content
- Quantifying the loss makes the lesson feel expensive (because it was)
- "Reading this costs you nothing" reframes the post as a gift

## 11. The "Harsh Truths" Listicle

### Description

A list where every item is something the audience needs to hear but won't enjoy. Tough love as a format. The discomfort is the share-trigger.

### Example

```
5 harsh truths about building an audience:

1. Your first 100 posts are practice. Nobody's watching. That's a gift.

2. People follow you for one topic. Post about everything, grow at nothing.

3. Consistency won't make you good. It makes you findable when you get good.

4. Your dream clients read and never engage. Silence isn't failure.

5. You're one year of discomfort away from a completely different life.

Most people can't handle #5.
That's why it works.
```

### Template

```
[N] harsh truths about [topic]:

1. [Truth — uncomfortable claim + one softening/explaining line]

2. [Truth]

3. [Truth]

...

Most people can't handle #[X].
[One-line kicker about why]
```

### How It Works

- "Harsh truths" pre-frames discomfort as honesty, disarming defensiveness
- Each truth pairs a slap with an explanation — hurt then help
- Calling out one item at the end drives comment debate on that number
- Truths must be earned observations, not recycled motivational lines

## 12. The "Breakdown" Post

### Description

Analytical deep-dive: deconstructs why something successful works (a creator, a landing page, a pricing model). You do the studying; readers get the synthesis.

### Example

```
I studied 50 viral LinkedIn posts from the past 90 days.

43 of them shared the same 4 traits:

1. Hook under 12 words
The fold is brutal. Long hooks die unread.

2. A number in the first line
Specificity signals substance before you've read a word.

3. One idea, zero tangents
Every viral post passes the "explain it in one sentence" test.

4. White space as design
Average paragraph: 1.4 lines. Reading feels like sliding.

None of this is talent.
All of it is pattern recognition.
```

### Template

```
I studied [N] [successful examples] from [timeframe/source].

[Most] shared the same [N] traits:

1. [Trait]
[One line of evidence or mechanism]

2. [Trait]
[One line]

...

None of this is [assumed cause — talent/luck].
All of it is [real cause — learnable].
```

### How It Works

- The sample size ("I studied 50...") is the credibility engine
- Findings framed as traits/patterns feel like research, not opinion
- Evidence lines under each trait separate analysis from assertion
- "Learnable, not talent" closing converts insight into encouragement

## 13. The "Daily Routine / Day in the Life" Post

### Description

Shows the unglamorous reality behind a result. Anti-hustle-porn: the power move is honesty about how boring an effective day looks.

### Example

```
My daily schedule as a $2M/year solopreneur:

5:30am — Wake up. No alarm hacking, I just sleep early.
6:00am — Write for 90 minutes. This is the whole business.
8:00am — Gym.
9:30am — Ship one thing: post, email, or product tweak.
12:00pm — Lunch. Long one.
1:00pm — Calls (Tuesday/Thursday only).
3:00pm — Done.

No 4am cold plunges.
No 16-hour grind.

Just 90 focused minutes that actually matter,
protected like my life depends on it.

Because it does.
```

### Template

```
My daily schedule as [credible identity]:

[Time] — [Activity + honest note]
[Time] — [Activity]
[Time] — [Activity]
...
[Time] — Done.

No [hustle cliché].
No [hustle cliché].

Just [the one thing that actually matters], protected.
```

### How It Works

- Time-stamped format is inherently scannable and voyeuristic
- Honest notes ("Long one.") build more trust than optimization theater
- Rejecting hustle clichés positions you against a shared enemy
- Isolating the ONE leverage activity is the actual lesson

## 14. The "One-Liner Ladder" Post

### Description

A sequence of short parallel lines that escalate to a reframe. No story, no list numbers — pure rhythm. Welsh uses these between heavier posts.

### Example

```
Amateurs wait for motivation.
Professionals follow a schedule.

Amateurs need perfect conditions.
Professionals work in the margins.

Amateurs quit when it stops being fun.
Professionals know it was never about fun.

The gap isn't talent.
It's a decision you make daily.
```

### Template

```
[Group A] [behavior].
[Group B] [contrasting behavior].

[Group A] [behavior].
[Group B] [contrasting behavior].

[Group A] [behavior].
[Group B] [contrasting behavior].

The gap isn't [assumed cause].
It's [real cause].
```

### How It Works

- Parallel structure creates rhythm that pulls readers down the page
- Three contrast pairs is the sweet spot — two feels thin, five drags
- The final reframe must break the pattern to land
- Low effort to produce, ideal for maintaining daily cadence
- ⚠️ Highest slop-risk template here — check DNA anti-patterns before using (see below)

## 15. The "Ask Me Anything / Give First" Post

### Description

Engagement-first post: offers your expertise or a resource directly in the comments. The post is small; the comment section is the content.

### Example

```
I've built landing pages that converted at 40%+.

For the next 24 hours, drop your landing page below
and I'll reply with one specific improvement.

No pitch. No "DM me."
Just one useful fix per page.

Go.
```

### Template

```
[One-line credential relevant to the offer]

For the next [time window], [what readers should drop in comments]
and I'll [specific thing you'll give each one].

No pitch. No catch.

Go.
```

### How It Works

- Comment volume is the strongest algorithmic signal — this manufactures it
- The time window creates urgency; the "no pitch" line removes suspicion
- Every reply you write doubles as public proof of expertise
- Only run this when you can actually fulfill — broken promises burn trust

## Voice DNA Conflicts (read before executing)

Several Welsh stylistic tics collide with the anti-patterns in `voices/bui-thang/dna.md` and project DNA files. **Keep the template's skeleton, drop the tic:**

| Welsh tic | DNA anti-pattern it violates | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Slogan closers ("Visibility compounds. Perfectionism doesn't.") | Slogan closing / symmetrical insight | End with one concrete implication or next step |
| Perfect two-part symmetry ("Amateurs X. Professionals Y.") | Avoid perfect symmetry | Break the parallel on the final pair; make one side concrete |
| "Here's the thing:" / "Read that again." | Theatrical bucket brigade | Cut it; let the line stand |
| Motivational kickers ("You're one decision away.") | Motivational filler | Replace with evidence or delete |
| Announcing the payoff ("This changed everything:") | Announce importance | Show the change with a number or example |

The templates' *structural* strengths — dated contrasts, milestone anchors, time-boxed systems, sample-size claims, price-tagged mistakes — carry the value. Those survive any voice. The tics don't need to.
